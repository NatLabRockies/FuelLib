Correlations and Mixture Rules

Group contribution methods and reference data give the properties of each compound at standard conditions (see Fuel Property Prediction Framework). This page presents the correlations that evaluate the properties of each compound at other temperatures and pressures, and the mixture rules that combine the compound properties into the properties of the fuel. Unless noted otherwise in the individual correlation, all units are assumed to be SI: length (m), mass (kg), time (s), temperature (K), mole (mol).

Notation

A tilde denotes a molar property (e.g., \(\tilde{C}_{p,i}\) in J/mol/K and \(\Delta\tilde{H}_{v,i}\) in J/mol), while the same symbol without a tilde denotes the corresponding mass-based property (e.g., \(C_{p,i}\) in J/kg/K and \(\Delta H_{v,i}\) in J/kg). The subscript i denotes the i-th compound in a mixture.

Individual component properties (fuellib.correlate.components)

This section presents correlations for physical properties that leverage the individual compound properties from the Group Contribution Methods and the reference data. These correlations make it possible to evaluate physical properties at non-standard temperatures and pressures, given that group contribution properties are only defined at standard conditions. The functions are in fuellib.correlate.components and are available as methods of Fuel.comps. The Reduced temperature quantities are used throughout this section for each compound i, provided \(T\) in K unless noted otherwise.

Kinematic viscosity

Wrapped by fuel.comps.nu, where the correlation argument selects "Nannoolal" (default) or "Dutt".

Divides equation (6) from Nannoolal (2009) by density.

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to calculate the kinematic viscosities.

Returns:

Component kinematic viscosities in m^2/s.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

fuellib.correlate.components.viscosity_kinematic_dutt(fuel, temp)[source]

Calculate the kinematic viscosity using Dutt’s equation.

Uses Dutt’s equation (4.23) from “Viscosity of Liquids”. The equation predicts viscosity in mm^2/s and is converted to SI units.

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to compute property.

Returns:

Viscosity of each component in m^2/s.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

The kinematic viscosity of the i-th compound of the fuel,

\[\nu_i = \frac{\mu_i}{\rho_i},\]

is calculated with the GCM of Nannoolal et al.[2] (default) or from Dutt’s equation (Eq. 4.23 in Viscosity of Liquids[1]). Dutt’s equation calculates the kinematic viscosity directly provided \(T\) in \(^{\circ}\) C:

\[\nu_i = 10^{-6} \times \exp \bigg\{-3.0171 + \frac{442.78 + 1.6452 \,T_{b,i}}{T + 239 - 0.19 \,T_{b,i}} \bigg\}.\]

For supported hydrocarbons, the Nannoolal GCM calculates the dynamic viscosity of the i-th compound as:

\[\mu_i = 1.3\times10^{-3}\exp\left[-dBv_i\frac{T-T_{v,i}}{T-(T_{v,i}/16)}\right],\]

then obtains \(\nu_i = \mu_i / \rho_i\). The compound parameters \(dBv_i\) and \(T_{v,i}\) are calculated from the Nannoolal group contributions (see Equations for Nannoolal properties).

fuellib.correlate.components.enthalpy_vaporization_mass(fuel, temp)[source]

Calculate mass-based enthalpy of vaporization adjusted for temperature.

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to compute property.

Returns:

Mass-bass enthalpy of vaporization in J/kg.

Return type:

The molar enthalpy of vaporization for each compound at standard pressure and temperature, \(\Delta\tilde{H}_{v,\textit{stp},i}\), is adjusted for variations in temperature[3]:

\[\Delta\tilde{H}_{v,i} = \Delta\tilde{H}_{v,\textit{stp},i} \bigg(\frac{1 - T_{r,i}}{1-T_{r,b,i}} \bigg)^{0.38}.\]

The mass-based enthalpy of vaporization (basis="mass", default) is obtained by dividing by the molecular weight:

\[\Delta H_{v,i} = \frac{\Delta\tilde{H}_{v,i}}{M_{w,i}}.\]

Liquid molar volume

Wrapped by fuel.comps.vm.

fuellib.correlate.components.molar_liquid_volume(fuel, temp)[source]

Compute molar liquid volume with temperature correction.

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to compute property.

Returns:

Molar liquid volume in m^3/mol.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

The liquid molar volume is calculated at a specific temperature \(T\) using the generalized Rackett equation[4] [5] with an updated \(\phi_i\) parameter[3]:

\[V_{m,i} = V_{m,\textit{stp},i} Z^{\phi_i}_{c,i},\]

where

\[\begin{split}Z_{c,i} &= 0.29056 - 0.08775 \omega_i, \\ \phi_i &= \begin{cases} (1 - T_{r,i})^{2/7} - (1 - T_{r,\textit{stp},i})^{2/7}, & \text{ if } T \leq T_{c,i} \\ - (1 - T_{r,\textit{stp},i})^{2/7}, & \text{ if } T > T_{c,i} \end{cases}. \label{eq:phi}\end{split}\]

The molar specific heat capacity, \(\tilde{C}_{p,i}\) (basis="molar"), is evaluated from the group contribution expansion in \(\theta = (T - 298)/700\) (see Equations for Constantinou and Gani properties). The mass specific heat capacity (basis="mass", default) is obtained by dividing by the molecular weight:

\[C_{p,i} = \dfrac{\tilde{C}_{p,i}}{M_{w,i}}\]

Alternatively, the Ruzicka-Domalski[7] group additivity correlation is available, with \(\theta = T / 100\) (K) and the rd_A, rd_B, and rd_D properties included in the gani GCM (see Constantinou and Gani):

\[\tilde{C}_{p,i} = R \big(A_i + B_i \theta + D_i \theta^2\big).\]

Vapor pressure

Wrapped by fuel.comps.pvap, where the correlation argument selects "Lee-Kesler" (default) or "Ambrose-Walton".

fuellib.correlate.components.vapor_pressure(fuel, temp, *, correlation='Lee-Kesler')[source]

Compute vapor pressure.

Can use Ambrose-Walton or Lee-Kesler correlations (default Lee-Kesler).

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to compute property.

  • correlation (Literal['Ambrose-Walton', 'Lee-Kesler']) – Correlation method (“Ambrose-Walton” or “Lee-Kesler”).

Returns:

Vapor pressure in Pa.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

The vapor pressure for each compound is calculated as a function of temperature using either the Lee–Kesler method[8] or the Ambrose-Walton method[9]. Both methods solve

\[\ln p_{r,v,i} = f_i^{(0)} + \omega_i f_i^{(1)} + \omega_i^2 f_i^{(2)}\]

for the reduced vapor pressure for each compound, \(p_{r,v,i} = p_{v,i}/p_{c,i}\). The default method in FuelLib is the Lee-Kesler method, as it is more stable at higher temperatures. The Lee-Kesler[8] method defines

\[\begin{split}f_i^{(0)} &= 5.92714 - \frac{6.09648}{T_{r,i}} - 1.28862 \ln T_{r,i} + 0.169347 \, T_{r,i}^6, \\ f_i^{(1)} &= 15.2518 - \frac{15.6875}{T_{r,i}} - 13.4721 \ln T_{r,i} + 0.43577 \, T_{r,i}^6, \\ f_i^{(2)} &= 0,\end{split}\]

The Ambrose-Walton[9] correlation sets:

\[\begin{split}f_i^{(0)} &= \frac{- 5.97616\tau_i + 1.29874\tau_i^{1.5} - 0.60394\tau_i^{2.5} - 1.06841\tau_i^{5}}{T_{r,i}}, \\ f_i^{(1)} &= \frac{- 5.03365\tau_i + 1.11505\tau_i^{1.5} - 5.41217\tau_i^{2.5} - 7.46628\tau_i^{5},}{T_{r,i}}, \\ f_i^{(2)} &= \frac{- 0.64771\tau_i + 2.41539\tau_i^{1.5} - 4.26979\tau_i^{2.5} - 3.25259\tau_i^{5}}{T_{r,i}},\end{split}\]

with \(\tau_i = 1 - T_{r,i}\).

Vapor pressure (Antoine coefficients)

Wrapped by fuel.comps.pvap_antoine_coeffs.

fuellib.correlate.components.vapor_pressure_antoine_coeffs(fuel, temp_vals=None, *, units='mks', correlation='Lee-Kesler')[source]

Estimate Antoine coefficients for vapor pressure of an individual compound.

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp_vals (Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]] | None) – Temperature range or nodes for Antoine fit in Kelvin. Defaults to [273.15, Tb_i].

  • units (Literal['mks', 'cgs', 'dyne/cm^2', 'Pa']) – Units for pressure in fit (“mks”, “cgs”, “dyne/cm^2”, “Pa”). Defaults to “mks”.

  • correlation (Literal['Ambrose-Walton', 'Lee-Kesler']) – Correlation method (“Ambrose-Walton” or “Lee-Kesler”). Defaults to “Lee-Kesler”.

Returns:

Coefficients A, B, C, D for each compound.

Raises:

ValueError – If units or temp_vals are invalid.

Return type:

tuple[ndarray[tuple[int]], ndarray[tuple[int]], ndarray[tuple[int]], ndarray[tuple[int]]]

Users also have the option to return the coefficients from an Antoine fit based on the mixture vapor pressure calculated from Raoult’s law above. Antoine’s equation is:

\[\log_{10}\Big(\frac{p_{v,i}}{D_i}\Big) = A_i - \frac{B_i}{C_i + T},\]

where \(D_i\) is a conversion factor for converting \(p_{v,i}\) from Pa to cgs-based units dyne/cm 2 (\(D_i = 10\)). This feature was added to provide Pele users an option to estimate Antoine coefficients for use in CFD simulations with spray. See the PelePhysics documentation for additional information.

Surface tension

Wrapped by fuel.comps.sigma, where the correlation argument selects "Brock-Bird" (default) or "Pitzer".

fuellib.correlate.components.surface_tension(fuel, temp, correlation='Brock-Bird')[source]

Calculate surface tension of each compound at a given temperature.

Uses Brock-Bird (default) or Pitzer correlations (Poling 12-3.5, 12-3.7).

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to compute property.

  • correlation (Literal['Brock-Bird', 'Pitzer']) – Correlation method (“Brock-Bird” or “Pitzer”). Default is “Brock-Bird”.

Returns:

Surface tension in N/m.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

Surface tension for each compound is approximated using the relation:

\[\sigma_i = p_{c,i}^{2/3} T_{c,i}^{1/3} Q_i (1 - T_{r,i})^{11/9},\]

provided \(p_{c,i}\) in bar. The \(Q_i\) term is defined by Brock and Bird[10] (default in FuelLib) as

\[Q_i = 0.1196 \bigg[1 + \frac{T_{r,b,i} \log(p_{c,i}/1.01325)}{1 - T_{r,b,i}}\bigg] - 0.279,\]

or by Curl and Pitzer[11] [14] [15] as

\[Q_i = \frac{1.86 + 1.18 \omega_i}{19.05} \bigg[ \frac{3.75 + 0.91 \omega_i}{0.291 - 0.08\omega_i} \bigg]^{2/3}.\]

Thermal conductivity

Wrapped by fuel.comps.k.

fuellib.correlate.components.thermal_conductivity_latini(fuel, temp)[source]

Calculate thermal conductivity at a given temperature.

Uses Latini et al. method (Poling equation 10-9.1).

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • temp (Quantity[float]) – Temperature to compute property.

Returns:

Thermal conductivity in W/m/K.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

Thermal conductivity for each compound is computed according to the method of Latini et al. as summarized in Poling’s[11] book:

\[k_i = \frac{A_i(1 - T_{r,i})^{0.38}}{T_{r,i}^{1/6}}.\]

The constant \(A_i\) is defined by:

\[A_i = \frac{A^\ast T_{b,i}^\alpha}{M_{w,i}^\beta T_{c,i}^{\gamma}},\]

provided \(M_{w,i}\) in g/mol. The exponents vary depending on the family of the compound as defined in Thermal conductivity relation parameters. It is assumed that:

  • aromatics have contain aromatic group contributions (e.g. ACCH)

  • cycloparaffins contain a ring (e.g. 5-membered ring) and do not contain aromatic groups

  • olefins contain one or more pairs of carbon atoms linked by a double bond and do not contain aromatic groups or rings

  • all other compounds are assumed to be saturated hydrocarbons.

These structural checks are performed on each compound’s RDKit Mol object (built from its SMILES string via from_smiles()) using has_aromatic(), has_ring(), and has_double_bond() from fuellib.rdk.mol.

fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

Returns:

Component flash points in K.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

fuellib.correlate.components.flash_point_alibakhshi(fuel)[source]

Calculate component flash points with Alibakhshi (2017) correlation eq. (5).

Parameters:

fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

Returns:

Component flash points in K.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

Two correlations are available for the flash point \(T_{\textit{flash},i}\) of each compound, both based on the normal boiling point \(T_{b,i}\). The Alqaheem and Riazi[12] correlation takes the flash point as a fixed fraction of the boiling point:

\[T_{\textit{flash},i} = 0.70 \, T_{b,i}.\]

The modified group-contribution correlation of Alibakhshi et al.[13] adds a group-contribution term:

\[T_{\textit{flash},i} = 12.14 + 0.73 \, T_{b,i} + \sum_{k=1}^{N_{g_1}} \mathbf{N}_{ik} \phi_k,\]

where \(\phi_k\) are the group contributions registered as the alibakhshi_phi property of the gani GCM (see Constantinou and Gani).

Lower heating value

fuellib.correlate.components.lower_heating_value(fuel)[source]

Calculate component lower heating values from a Hess cycle.

This estimate corresponds to hydrocarbons only; it does not account for heteroatom-containing groups. The result is an engineering estimate related to ASTM D4809/D3338 heating-value characterization, not a simulated bomb-calorimeter test.

Parameters:

fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

Returns:

Lower heating values of the components in J/kg.

Return type:

Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]]

The lower (net) heating value \(LHV_i\) is computed from a Hess cycle using the enthalpy of formation \(\Delta\tilde{H}_{f,i}\) and enthalpy of vaporization \(\Delta\tilde{H}_{v,\textit{stp},i}\). The liquid-phase enthalpy of formation is

\[\Delta\tilde{H}_{f,\ell,i} = \Delta\tilde{H}_{f,i} - \Delta\tilde{H}_{v,\textit{stp},i},\]

and the molar enthalpy of combustion (an exothermic, i.e. negative, quantity in J/mol) of a compound with \(n_{C,i}\) carbon and \(n_{H,i}\) hydrogen atoms (from its RDKit molecule) is

\[\Delta\tilde{H}_{c,i} = n_{C,i} \, \Delta\tilde{H}_f^{\text{CO}_2(g)} + \frac{n_{H,i}}{2} \, \Delta\tilde{H}_f^{\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)} - \Delta\tilde{H}_{f,\ell,i},\]

with \(\Delta\tilde{H}_f^{\text{CO}_2(g)} = -393.51\) kJ/mol and \(\Delta\tilde{H}_f^{\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)} = -241.83\) kJ/mol at 298.15 K. The lower heating value is then

\[LHV_i = -\frac{\Delta\tilde{H}_{c,i}}{M_{w,i}}.\]

This estimate is valid for hydrocarbons only and does not account for heteroatom-containing groups.

While many of the mixture properties in FuelLib have a unique mixing rule, FuelLib’s arithmetic and geometric functions provide general mixing rules based on the suggestions of Harstad et al[16]. For a given property \(Q\)

\[Q = \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \sum_{j=1}^{N_c} X_i X_j Q_{ij},\]

where the pseudo-property for the couple of components, \(Q_{ij}\) is computed using an arithmetic,

\[Q_{ij} = \frac{Q_i + Q_j}{2},\]

or a geometric mean,

\[Q_{ij} = \sqrt{Q_i \cdot Q_j},\]

where \(Q_i\) is the property of the i-th compound of the multicomponent mixture.

Mixture freeze point

Wrapped by fuel.mix.t_freeze.

fuellib.correlate.mixture.freeze_point_boehm(fuel, yi=None, *, alpha=1.0)[source]

Calculate the freeze point of the mixture using Boehm (2022) eq. (21).

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • yi (Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]] | None) – Mass fractions of each compound in the mixture. Defaults to fuel.y0 (initial mass fractions).

  • alpha (float) – Scaling applied to the ideal mixing-entropy term. Defaults to 1.0 (classical ideal-solution entropy term).

Returns:

Mixture freeze point in K.

Raises:

RuntimeError – If the root finder fails to converge.

Return type:

Quantity[float]

The freeze point of the mixture is estimated with the solid-liquid equilibrium model of Boehm et al.[21], equation 21. For each compound \(j\) with mole fraction \(X_j\), the candidate freeze temperature \(T_j\) solves

\[T_j \Big[\Delta S_{\textit{fus},j} + X_j \Delta\tilde{C}_{p,j} \ln\frac{T_j}{T_{m,j}} + \alpha \, \Delta S_{\textit{mix},j}\Big] = \Delta H_{\textit{fus},j} + X_j \Delta\tilde{C}_{p,j} (T_{m,j} - T_j),\]

where

\[\begin{split}\Delta S_{\textit{mix},j} &= -\frac{R}{X_j}\big[(1 - X_j)\ln(1 - X_j) + X_j \ln X_j\big], \\ \Delta H_{\textit{fus},j} &= T_{m,j} \, \Delta S_{\textit{fus},j}, \\ \Delta\tilde{C}_{p,j} &= -0.35 \, \tilde{C}_{p,\textit{stp},j},\end{split}\]

\(T_{m,j}\) is the melting point from tm (a reference value when available, otherwise the Constantinou-Gani prediction; see Reference Property Data), \(\Delta S_{\textit{fus},j}\) is provided by the boehm GCM (see Boehm), and \(\alpha\) is an optional scaling of the ideal mixing-entropy term (default 1). The mixture freeze point is the highest \(T_j\) among compounds with \(X_j > 10^{-6}\), i.e. the temperature at which the first crystal forms on cooling. This is an equilibrium screening model that does not capture cooling rate, supercooling, or solid-phase nonideality.

The mixture heat of combustion \(\Delta H^\circ_c\) is reported per unit mass as the positive magnitude of the heat released, and is computed as the mass-fraction weighted average of the component lower heating values \(LHV_i\) (see Lower heating value). The yield sooting index \(YSI\) is a dimensionless measure of a fuel’s tendency to soot. Mixture derived cetane number

Wrapped by fuel.mix.dcn.

fuellib.correlate.mixture.derived_cetane_number(fuel, yi=None, *, temp_ref=Quantity(298.15, 'kelvin'))[source]

Calculate the derived cetane number (DCN) of the fuel mixture.

Combines each component’s derived cetane number with a linear, liquid volume fraction mixing rule. Volume fractions are derived from mass fractions using component liquid densities at temp_ref.

Parameters:

  • fuel (Fuel) – Fuel object.

  • yi (Quantity[ndarray[tuple[int]]] | None) – Mass fractions of each compound in the mixture. Defaults to fuel.y0 (initial mass fractions).

  • temp_ref (Quantity[float]) – Reference temperature for liquid densities. Required for converting mass fractions to volume fractions.

Returns:

Derived cetane number of the mixture.

Raises:

ValueError – If derived cetane number is unavailable for any component.

Return type:

Quantity[float]

The derived cetane number \(DCN\) is a dimensionless measure of ignition quality. The component values \(DCN_i\) are reference data from ref_compounds.csv and are available as dcn (see Reference Property Data); they are not predicted by FuelLib. The mixture value is computed with a linear liquid volume-fraction mixing rule,

\[DCN = \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \phi_i \, DCN_i, \qquad \phi_i = \frac{Y_i / \rho_i}{\sum_{k=1}^{N_c} Y_k / \rho_k},\]

where \(\phi_i\) is the liquid volume fraction of compound i and \(\rho_i\) is the component liquid density evaluated at a reference temperature temp_ref (default: standard temperature, constants.T_STP). A ValueError listing the compounds is raised if the reference data are missing for any component.

Linear volume-fraction blending is a first-order approximation. Cetane number can blend nonlinearly for certain mixtures, depending on the chemical structures of the components[25], so the mixture \(DCN\) should be treated as an estimate. The graph neural network models of Kim et al. for predicting cetane numbers are available at BioE-KimLab/CNpred.
